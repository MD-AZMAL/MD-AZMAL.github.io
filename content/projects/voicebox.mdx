<Lead>
Voice chat looks like a solved problem until you try to build one. The audio is
the easy part — browsers hand you that. What you actually end up writing is a
signalling server, a room lifecycle, and a great deal of care about what happens
when somebody's network drops mid-sentence.
</Lead>

Voicebox is a small, complete answer to that: users create a voice room, share
the link, and talk. No accounts, no install.

## Shape of it

The split is deliberate and follows what each side is good at.

| Piece | Runs on | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Web client | Next.js on Vercel | Room UI, device permissions, peer connections |
| Signalling | Go | Room registry, peer discovery, offer/answer relay |
| Media | Browser to browser | Audio never touches the server |

Keeping media peer-to-peer is what makes the whole thing deployable on a free
tier. The Go service only ever moves small JSON messages, so it stays cheap and
boring under load — which is exactly what you want from the component that
everybody depends on.

<Callout tone="tip">
  The thing worth stealing from this project is the room lifecycle, not the
  WebRTC code. Deciding when a room exists, when it is empty, and who is allowed
  to resurrect it is where most of the real bugs live.
</Callout>

## What I took from it

Coming off two years of service-mesh and Kubernetes work at IMESH, this was a
deliberate change of altitude: no cluster, no control plane, just a protocol and
two processes. It was a useful reminder that most of the difficulty in
distributed systems is not scale — it is agreeing on state between parties that
can disappear without warning.

That problem does not go away when the parties are models instead of browsers.
