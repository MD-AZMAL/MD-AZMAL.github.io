<Lead>
Sharp Tune started from a small irritation: every desktop music player I tried
wanted to be a library manager, a store, or a streaming client. I wanted one
that opened a folder, played what was in it, and looked good doing it.
</Lead>

It is the project of mine that other people actually use — listed in the
Electron app directory, and the one that keeps collecting stars years after the
last commit.

## What it is

A native-feeling player for Windows and Linux, built on Electron, with an audio
visualiser driven by the Web Audio API. Point it at a directory; it plays.

## The visualiser

The visualiser is the reason the project exists and the reason people star it.
It taps the analyser node in the Web Audio graph, reads the frequency data each
frame, and draws it — which sounds trivial until you try to make it look
deliberate rather than like a level meter having a seizure.

Two decisions did most of the work:

- **Smooth between frames, not within them.** Raw FFT output is jittery.
  Interpolating each bar toward its new value, instead of snapping, is the
  difference between "technical" and "designed".
- **Pick a frequency range humans care about.** The top of the spectrum is
  mostly empty on real music. Spending pixels there wastes the display.

<Callout tone="tip">
  If you want to learn the Web Audio API, a visualiser is a better first project
  than an effects chain. You get immediate visual feedback on whether your
  understanding of the graph is correct.
</Callout>

## Why it is still here

Electron gets criticised, usually fairly, for shipping a browser to draw a
window. For this project it was exactly right: one codebase, two platforms, and
direct access to the audio APIs I needed. The alternative was writing the same
player twice and getting the visualiser wrong in two languages.

Choosing the heavier tool because it makes the *interesting* part of the work
tractable is a trade I have made many times since, generally without regret.
