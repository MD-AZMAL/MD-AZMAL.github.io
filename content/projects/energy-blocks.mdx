<Lead>
On a micro-grid, two neighbours trading electricity is not an accounting
problem. It is a control problem wearing accounting's clothes. Supply and demand
have to meet at the right frequency, and a ledger that records only money has
recorded only half of what happened.
</Lead>

Energy Blocks came out of a cybersecurity research internship at the **C3i
Center, IIT Kanpur**, where the question was whether a permissioned ledger could
make power sharing between households both verifiable and billable.

## Why the frequency is on the ledger

A transaction here is a transfer of kilowatt-hours *at a frequency band*. That
second half is the interesting part. Grid frequency is the shared signal that
tells you whether the system is balanced: drifting low means demand is outrunning
supply. If the band is recorded alongside the energy, then billing and grid
health are answerable from the same source of truth, and nobody has to be
trusted to report either one honestly afterwards.

## Why Fabric

The first version of this idea ([Electrify](https://github.com/MD-AZMAL/Electrify)) ran on a
blockchain engine I wrote from scratch, and it was the right way to learn the
problem. It was the wrong way to run it.

<Compare>
  <Side kind="bad" label="from scratch">
    Proof-of-work across four nodes is theatre. Clock drift between small devices
    makes block ordering ambiguous. Gossip without back-pressure floods the
    slowest participant first.
  </Side>
  <Side kind="good" label="Hyperledger Fabric">
    Permissioned membership, a pluggable ordering service, and chaincode that
    can be unit-tested like ordinary code. All the properties the toy version
    was silently missing.
  </Side>
</Compare>

Writing the naive engine first was still worth it. It told me which properties I
actually needed before a framework hid them from me — which is the same reason I
now prefer to write a thin version of any runtime before adopting one.
