<Lead>
A honeypot is a lie you leave running. Shawpot listens on an HTTP port, serves
something that looks like a forgotten admin panel, and records everything about
whoever arrives — headers, timing, and the order they try paths in.
</Lead>

It is a few hundred lines of Python. The interesting part was never the code; it
was what a month of logs does to how you read a request.

## Three kinds of visitor

Traffic to an exposed port sorts itself into shapes surprisingly quickly:

- **Scanners** hit one path and leave. This is almost everything.
- **Scripts** walk a wordlist in order, at machine speed, and never deviate.
- **People** pause. They change user-agent and come back slower. There are very
  few of them.

> The interesting attackers are the ones who behave like they are being watched.

## What it changed

Before Shawpot I read logs as evidence: this request, was it malicious? After
it, I read them as behaviour. The gap *between* requests carried more
information than any individual request did — a wordlist walked at a uniform
120ms is a different actor from one walked at a human's irregular pace, even
when the requests are byte-identical.

## Running one responsibly

A honeypot is a system you have deliberately made attractive to attackers, which
makes it a system you have deliberately made into a liability.

1. **Low interaction, on purpose.** It serves a convincing surface and nothing
   more. There is no shell to escape into, because there is no shell.
2. **Isolate it.** Its own host, its own network, nothing trusted reachable from it.
3. **Treat the logs as untrusted input.** They are attacker-controlled strings.
   Rendering them unescaped in your own dashboard is how the honeypot ends up
   attacking you.

There is also a Ruby sibling, `shawpot-init`, from when I was curious whether
the same design read differently in another language. It did, slightly, and not
in Ruby's favour for this particular job.
